# DXA or InBody? What a Body Scan Can and Can't Tell You

*A side finding from building [Helse](/blog/helse).*

While teaching Helse to read body scans, I ended up with two reports on my desk: my own DXA, and my husband's InBody. Same idea — how much of you is fat, muscle and bone — and almost nothing else in common. So I went and checked what each one can actually tell you.

**The short version: DXA is the better scan. InBody is the better habit.** And whichever you use, how you show up for it matters more than the brand.

| | **DXA (DEXA)** | **InBody** (bioimpedance) |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Two low-dose X-ray beams tell bone, fat and lean tissue apart | A small electrical current reads your body water; fat and muscle are calculated from it |
| One person, one number | The standard other scans are checked against | Can be several points off — usually low on fat |
| Bone density | Measured | Not measured |
| Visceral fat (around the organs) | Estimated from the image, and it tracks CT well | Estimated from formulas, and it doesn't match DXA |
| Radiation | Less than a day of natural background | None |
| Best for | An accurate picture, once or twice a year | Frequent check-ins, as a trend |

## How far apart they read

The best comparison I found is recent and large: [1,000 adults scanned on both](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-025-01664-4) — an InBody 770 and a DXA — with meals and exercise left uncontrolled, the way most people actually get scanned. On average the InBody put body fat **2.8 points lower in women and 4.2 lower in men**. For any one person it could land anywhere from about 8 points lower to 2 points higher than the DXA in women, and 10 lower to 2 higher in men. Its visceral-fat figure was the one measure that simply didn't line up.

A [smaller, controlled study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocd.2018.10.008) found the same direction: three InBody models all underestimated fat and overestimated lean mass compared with DXA — though with little error for each individual once the bias was known.

So an InBody reading of 22% body fat and a DXA reading of 26% aren't necessarily a contradiction. They may be the same body.

## Same scan, different day: why it can disagree with itself

Both machines are sensitive to what you did that morning. In one study, athletes were [scanned fasted in the morning and again after a normal day](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-018-3861-8) of eating, drinking and moving, and again six months later. When the conditions didn't match, the six-month changes came out bigger — and **"often in the opposite direction"** to the real ones. Impedance was the most affected, but no method was immune.

That's the whole game for tracking: same machine, same time of day, same conditions, every time.

## How big a change is real

Scanned twice a week apart, people's DXA body fat typically agreed [within about a point](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-025-01664-4); the InBody a little less closely. For the InBody, the controlled study put the [smallest real change at 2–2.7 points](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocd.2018.10.008) of body fat.

A rough rule: on the same DXA, a change of more than about 2 points is probably real; on an InBody, about 3. Anything smaller is the machine, not you. It's the same idea Helse uses for blood tests — every marker wobbles a little between draws, and only a move bigger than its normal wobble counts as a change.

## Same brand, same machine

Even two DXAs don't agree perfectly. Scanned on both a Hologic and a GE machine within a week, people's body fat [differed by 1.4 points on average](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.1654) — close to the size of a real change. A European consensus on muscle loss puts it bluntly: [different DXA brands "do not give consistent results"](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169). If you switch clinics, start a new baseline.

## What to look at on a DXA report

A DXA report is dense — mine ran to four pages, one of them sideways. The professional body for DXA recommends every report include [BMI, bone density, total fat, total lean and body fat %](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocd.2013.08.018), with visceral fat and a muscle index as optional extras. Here's what I'd read first:

- **Body fat %** — the headline number, and the one to compare over time on the same machine.
- **Visceral fat (VAT)** — the fat packed around your organs, which [predicts mortality better](https://doi.org/10.21037/qims.2020.03.02) than the fat under your skin. DXA's estimate correlates closely with a CT scan's (r = 0.93).
- **Appendicular lean mass index** — the lean mass in your arms and legs, divided by height². In older adults, the European sarcopenia definition flags [below 5.5 kg/m² in women and 7.0 in men](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169) as low muscle — alongside a strength test, not on its own. The same DXA society notes such thresholds [still need validating](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocd.2013.08.018), so treat it as a direction, not a verdict.
- **Bone density and its Z-score** — how your bones compare with people your age. Before menopause, the [Z-score is the one to read](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/), and −2.0 or lower is "below the expected range for age." One caveat: a whole-body scan isn't the test for osteoporosis — that measures your [spine and hip](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/). If the number worries you, that's the scan to ask for.

What I skip: the per-limb tables, the percentiles, and the calories-per-activity page. Some of it is interesting; none of it is worth tracking.

## Reading one report

Here's how that plays out on a real-looking report — a composite, with invented numbers, but a shape I've now seen more than once: a woman of 38, 5 ft 6 in, 136 lb, BMI 22, scanned on a Hologic machine.

**Body fat 27.4%.** The report's own percentiles put her at the 22nd for women her age — leaner than three in four. And it's carried where it does the least harm: her trunk is 22% fat, her legs 34%, and her android/gynoid ratio is 0.84 (a ratio above 1, the belly-heavy pattern, is the one [linked to insulin resistance](https://doi.org/10.21037/qims.2020.03.02)). Her visceral fat is estimated at 61 cm². Everything about fat says: fine.

**Bone density 1.08 g/cm², Z-score −0.4.** Average for her age; anything above −2.0 is ["within the expected range for age."](https://iscd.org/official-positions-2023/) The T-score is blank — correct for a woman before menopause.

**Appendicular lean mass index 5.8 kg/m², 21st percentile.** This is the number. Four in five women her age have more muscle in their arms and legs. She's above the [European cut-off for low muscle](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afy169) (5.5), but not by much — and at 38 it's a reserve question, not a diagnosis. Muscle is the thing that's hard to rebuild later and easy to build now. On a report where everything else says "fine," this is the one line that argues for a change.

**A small asymmetry:** left arm 39% fat, right arm 34%, with more muscle on the right. Handedness, almost certainly. Worth a mention to a trainer; not worth worrying about.

**Resting metabolic rate 1,390 kcal.** Calculated from her lean mass, not measured — a starting point for a nutrition plan, not a fact about her.

So the whole report comes down to one sentence: *lean, average bones, light on muscle — and the muscle is what to work on.* That's what a body scan is for. Not the 45 numbers; the one that changes what you do next.

## What an InBody is good for

It's quick, cheap, radiation-free and repeatable — ideal for checking in between DXAs, as long as you treat it as a trend line rather than a precise number. It also reports body water measures a DXA doesn't. What I wouldn't lean on is its visceral-fat figure, or on comparing its body fat % with a DXA's.

## Radiation, and when not to scan

A whole-body DXA exposes you to about [4–5 microsieverts](https://doi.org/10.21037/qims.2020.03.02) — less than the 6.7 you get from natural background radiation in a single day. That's not a reason to scan monthly, but it's not a reason to avoid it either.

The exception is pregnancy: [tell the clinic](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/dexa) if there's any chance you're pregnant. A body-composition scan isn't medically necessary, so if there's any chance — for me, that means the weeks after an embryo transfer — I just wait. And since impedance works by reading body water, I also wouldn't trust an InBody in the middle of an IVF cycle.

## What I'd do

- **Once or twice a year: a DXA**, at the same clinic, on the same machine.
- **In between, if you want: an InBody**, as a trend.
- **Show up the same way every time**: first thing in the morning, before eating or exercising, after the bathroom. For a DXA, [skip calcium supplements for 24 hours](https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/dexa) beforehand.
- **Don't compare across machines** — not DXA with InBody, not one DXA brand with another.
- **Read changes against the machine's noise**: about 2 points of body fat on a DXA, about 3 on an InBody.
